## Why it works

The hero sentence "FIND YOUR WILDHOOD" is split into three oversized
slab-caps fragments and scattered across a single documentary photograph — a
father carrying a baby beside a parked RV, mountains behind — rather than set
as one headline block. Each word sits in open sky or negative space in the
photo, so the huge type never competes with the family subject; it reads as
environmental graphics painted onto the landscape, not text laid over a
picture. A diagonal crop cuts across the top-right corner of the hero image,
breaking the rectangle just enough to feel art-directed rather than a plain
photo box.

Below the fold the page switches to a flat sage-green ground and a completely
different rhythm: labelled photo tiles ("Find Your RV Match," "Find a Dealer")
connected by a thin wavy orange line threading beginner-path cards to
expert-path cards, turning a simple two-column list into a visual "journey"
metaphor without needing an literal roadmap illustration. Further down, the
page returns to full-bleed dark photography for a "Drive It / Tow It" pairing,
each with the same slab-caps treatment — so the poster-type hero device
recurs as a section marker throughout the page rather than a one-time hero
trick.

Colour discipline carries the persuasion: one warm orange accent (buttons, the
connector line, a small chevron pointer) against near-black photography and
sage-neutral panels. Nothing else competes for the eye, so the orange reads
instantly as "the next step."

## What to borrow (principle)

- **Split a headline into environmental fragments placed in the empty parts of
  a photograph** rather than stacking it as one block — the type becomes part
  of the scene instead of sitting on top of it.
- **Use a single accent colour as a wayfinding thread** — a thin coloured line
  connecting related cards does the job of an illustrated map with far less
  production.
- **Repeat the poster-type-over-photo device as a section marker** throughout
  the page, not just in the hero, so the identity stays legible on scroll.
- **Break one hero rectangle with a diagonal crop** to signal "designed," not
  "stock photo dropped in a box."

## What to leave

- Splitting the headline across the photo depends entirely on there being
  empty sky/ground in the right spots — this composition doesn't transfer to a
  busier photograph without reshooting or heavy cropping.
- The wavy connector line pattern, applied at real width, risks looking twee
  if used more than once per page.

## Brief

Build an outdoor-lifestyle hero on one full-bleed documentary photograph with
real people and real gear, no studio staging. Break the headline into two or
three oversized slab-caps fragments and place each one in an empty pocket of
the photograph (sky, ground, negative space) rather than as a single text
block. Crop one corner of the hero diagonally to avoid a plain rectangle.
Choose a single warm accent colour and use it exclusively — for CTA buttons, a
thin connector line threading related content cards together, and any pointer
chevrons. Ground supporting sections in a flat sage-neutral panel colour, and
return to full-bleed dark photography with the same slab-caps treatment as a
recurring section marker further down the page.
